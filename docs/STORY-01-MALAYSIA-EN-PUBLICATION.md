Not the first time. That is more or less the point.

This is my fourth long journey. At some point I stopped explaining it to myself. Life has too much to
offer — that is probably the whole reason I can't stop doing this.

One thing is different now. For the first time I am carrying a real camera. Semi-professional, nothing
spectacular, but a lot more than a phone. It changes more than I expected. I am not travelling and
taking pictures on the side any more. I stop where I used to keep walking.

It starts in Malaysia. It usually does.

I fly into Kuala Lumpur. From there I move on. To there I come back. Then I leave again. Three times
on this trip alone. On a map it is a hub. Over the years it has become something else as well — a
place I know my way around.

That goes back to 2014. Since then I meet friends here every time. Some of those connections have held
across years and across several journeys. It changes a country. You stop arriving as a complete
stranger, and you know that part of it will still be there when you come back.

Kuala Lumpur is loud. Huge, very urban, a lot of concrete. Sometimes dusty, sometimes completely wet.
And the city smells. Food, rain on hot asphalt, exhaust — usually all of it at the same time.

I find that impressive. Not pretty. Impressive.

What gets me every time is how visible the different communities are. Malay, Chinese and Indian
influences are the ones I see most clearly — not as a statistic, but in ordinary life. In the food. In
the streets. In the neighbourhoods. In the buildings. In people. A mosque, a Chinese temple, a Hindu
temple, a church, none of it far apart. Malaysia is more than those three influences. But those are
the ones I notice most, and the fact that it works side by side still impresses me.

The food comes out of that. For me the cooking is one of the real strengths of this country. You don't
get that mixture everywhere.

And Kuala Lumpur is movement. Airport. City. Friends. Food. Heat. Concrete. Rain. Then on.

George Town on Penang belongs to this too. If someone asks me where the best food in Malaysia is, I
say George Town. Eating there isn't a thing you tick off; it is how you understand the city. The
cultures sit close together and you can taste it. That sounds simple. It is exactly that simple.

I found Pulau Kapas by accident in 2016. Not because the island was on anybody's list — I just ended
up there and fell in love with it. It has been my favourite island since, and when I am in Malaysia I
try to get back.

What makes it is mostly what it doesn't have. Almost no infrastructure. Beach, sea, vegetation, a few
places to stay, people. Not much more than that. There are a few more buildings now than the first
time I came. The feeling has stayed.

Quiet. Beautiful. Clean. Small. Uncomplicated.

The reef looked good. Intact. The sea and the reef are a large part of why the island counts for me.

And the people on Kapas are great. I don't really need to say more than that.

I have known this country since 2014, but with the camera I see things I have walked past a hundred
times. In the city: concrete, density, people, weather. On Kapas the opposite — space, water, light, a
slower rhythm. No plan and no concept. Just looking more carefully.

Malaysia isn't a country I visited once. It is a starting point. Islands, cities, highlands with tea,
jungle, different religions, different communities — and from here the rest of Southeast Asia is right
there.

That is why the journey begins here. And why it carries on from here.
